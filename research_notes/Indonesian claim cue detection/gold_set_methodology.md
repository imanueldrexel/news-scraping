# Building and freezing an LLM-labelled, human-spot-checked gold set to measure recall of a cue-detection gate (causal / hedge / denial / end-point / plan cues, Indonesian news)

Scope note: all sample-size numbers in this file marked "computed" were calculated in this session with scipy (`scipy.stats.beta` for Clopper-Pearson, closed-form Wilson score), not taken from a paper. The formulas are standard; the reference for interval choice is Brown, Cai & DasGupta (2001), "Interval Estimation for a Binomial Proportion", Statistical Science 16(2) — [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/statistical-science/volume-16/issue-2/Interval-Estimation-for-a-Binomial-Proportion/10.1214/ss/1009213286.full) (cited from background knowledge; page not fetched this session).

## 1. How accurate are LLM annotators vs humans, including non-English / Indonesian, and what are their known biases?

### Takeaway
LLMs beat crowd workers on many English social-science labelling tasks, but they do not reliably match careful expert annotation or fine-tuned classifiers, quality is highly task- and dataset-dependent, and for Indonesian the one direct comparison found that humans still win on the more intricate tasks. Errors are systematic (model- and prompt-specific prevalence shifts), not random noise, so an LLM-labelled gold set must be validated against humans before it is trusted as ground truth for recall.

### Cited Findings
- Gilardi, Alizadeh & Kubli (PNAS 2023): on 4 samples of tweets and news articles (n = 6,183), zero-shot ChatGPT accuracy exceeded crowd workers (MTurk) by about 25 percentage points on average across relevance, stance, topic and frame detection; ChatGPT's intercoder agreement exceeded both crowd workers and trained annotators on all tasks; cost < $0.003 per annotation, ~30x cheaper than MTurk — [arXiv 2303.15056](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15056); [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10372638/)
  - Note: the comparison baseline is crowd workers, and "intercoder agreement" is the LLM agreeing with itself across runs, not accuracy against experts.
- Törnberg (2023): GPT-4 classifying US politicians' party affiliation from 2020 tweets achieved higher accuracy, higher reliability and equal or lower bias than expert classifiers and crowd workers, including on items requiring contextual inference — [arXiv 2304.06588](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06588)
  - Note: the task has objective ground truth (the account's party), which is easier to score than discourse-level cues.
- Ziems et al. (Computational Linguistics 2024, "Can Large Language Models Transform Computational Social Science?"): across many utterance-, conversation- and document-level CSS classification tasks, prompted LLMs, except in a minority of cases, do not match or exceed carefully fine-tuned classifiers, and "the best LLM performances are often too low to entirely replace human annotation"; they reach "fair" agreement with humans; recommended role is zero-shot annotator *within* a human team (iterative joint labelling) — [arXiv 2305.03514](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03514); [ACL Anthology CL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.cl-1.8.pdf)
- Pangakis, Wolken & Fasching (2023): replicating 27 annotation tasks across 11 social-science datasets with GPT-4, LLM performance is "promising but highly contingent on both the dataset and the type of annotation task"; they conclude any automated LLM annotation process "must validate the LLM's performance against labels generated by humans" and propose a validation workflow — [arXiv 2306.00176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00176) (per-task F1 figures not retrieved this session)
- Nasution & Onan (IEEE Access 2024, "ChatGPT Label"): compared human and LLM annotations for Turkish, Indonesian and Minangkabau on topic classification, tweet sentiment and emotion classification; LLM labels were competitive mainly on sentiment, while "human-generated annotations consistently outperformed LLM-generated ones in more intricate NLP tasks", attributed to LLM limits with context and ambiguity — [DOAJ record / DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2024.3402809](https://doaj.org/article/dad423f5ac434c06bcb34a89583c6d5a); [author repository](https://repository.uir.ac.id/24658/)
- A related Indonesian-tweet study reported in search snippets: GPT-4 reached macro-F1 0.84 on sentiment and emotion, slightly above human annotators, but Neutral sentiment and Fear emotion stayed hard (low agreement even among humans) — [repository.uir.ac.id record](https://repository.uir.ac.id/24931/) (snippet only; full text returned HTTP 403, so treat as unverified)
- Jadhav et al. (2024), Marathi (another low-resource language): GPT-4o and Llama 3.1 405B trailed fine-tuned BERT by 10.2 and 14.1 accuracy points on sentiment, news classification and hate speech — [arXiv 2411.17637](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17637v2)
- Stolwijk, Boukes & Trilling (2025, "Are generative AI text annotations systematically biased?"): across 20 generative LLM annotators, models bias annotations *systematically* and differently (for "rationality" in YouTube comments most models assigned higher prevalence than manual coders); prompt wording shifted prevalence estimates, with model choice the larger effect; selecting the model with the best F1 was *positively correlated* with more bias in downstream substantive findings — [arXiv 2512.08404](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08404v1)
- Abuse-detection work summarised in a 2026 survey: the same model can rate an implicitly abusive sentence harmful under one prompt template and neutral under another; instruction-tuned LLMs tend to under-predict positive classes while RLHF-tuned models (LLaMA-2-Chat, GPT-3.5) over-predict them; LLMs rely on surface cues, giving false positives on benign mentions and false negatives on euphemistic/indirect cases — [arXiv 2604.00323](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.00323) (search-snippet level; not fetched in full)
- Hedge detection specifically: on a human-annotated hedge gold standard (Roadrunner-Hedge corpus), a fine-tuned BERT beat zero- and few-shot GPT-4o and LLaMA-3; few-shot GPT-4o was second best — [likely arXiv 2408.03319](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03319v1) (search snippet only; the snippet was not tied to a URL with certainty and the page was not fetched, so verify the paper and its F1 before quoting)

### Inferences
- The gate's cue classes (causal connectives, hedges, denials, end-points, plans) are discourse/pragmatic phenomena closer to the "intricate" tasks where Indonesian LLM labels lagged humans than to sentiment, so expect LLM label error well above the ~5% seen on easier tasks — budget a real human check per cue class, not one pooled check.
- The two error directions matter differently for a recall audit: an LLM that *misses implicit cues* (e.g. causality without "karena/akibat", hedges carried by modal particles) removes hard positives from the gold set, so measured recall is inflated toward the gate's own surface-cue behaviour. An LLM that *over-labels* adds false positives that the gate rightly ignores, deflating measured recall (see section 5). Both must be checked.
- Because errors are systematic and prompt-sensitive, freeze the labelling prompt, model and model version with the gold set (record them in the gold set's sidecar), and treat a prompt change as producing a new gold set.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures LLM annotation accuracy for causal/hedge/denial cues *in Indonesian*. The closest evidence is Indonesian sentiment/emotion/topic (Nasution & Onan) and English hedge detection.
- Exact per-task numbers from Pangakis et al. and the hedge study could not be retrieved (abstract pages only).

## 2. Agreement metrics for skewed classes, thresholds, and how large a human spot-check must be

### Takeaway
For rare positive classes Cohen's kappa is unstable (the kappa paradoxes): report percent agreement plus positive-specific agreement (ppos / pneg, or simply the LLM label's precision and recall against the human) and, if a chance-corrected statistic is wanted, Gwet's AC1 or PABAK alongside kappa. A spot-check of 50 items per cue with zero errors only bounds that category's error rate at about 5.8% (one-sided 95%); 100 with zero errors bounds it at 3%.

### Cited Findings
- Kappa paradox 1 (Feinstein & Cicchetti): kappa is low despite high observed agreement when marginals are imbalanced; paradox 2: kappa is higher for asymmetrical than symmetrical imbalanced marginals; kappa's behaviour depends on prevalence — [Wongpakaran et al., BMC Med Res Methodol 2013](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1471-2288-13-61); [BMC Med Res Methodol 2014](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1471-2288-14-100)
- Fixes in that literature: PABAK (prevalence- and bias-adjusted kappa, Byrt et al.); Cicchetti & Feinstein's two separate indices ppos and pneg for positive and negative agreement, analogous to sensitivity/specificity; Gwet's AC1 is more stable than kappa under changing prevalence and stays close to percent agreement — [BMC Med Res Methodol 2013](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1471-2288-13-61); [PMC4236536](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4236536)
- Ziems et al. characterise LLM–human agreement on CSS tasks as "fair", i.e. not sufficient to replace humans — [arXiv 2305.03514](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03514)
- Computed (Clopper-Pearson one-sided 95% upper bound on the LLM's per-category label error rate, given e errors found in an n-item human spot-check):

  | n checked | 0 errors | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | 30 | 9.5% | 14.9% | 19.5% | 23.9% | 31.9% |
  | 50 | 5.8% | 9.1% | 12.1% | 14.8% | 19.9% |
  | 100 | 3.0% | 4.7% | 6.2% | 7.6% | 10.2% |
  | 200 | 1.5% | 2.3% | 3.1% | 3.8% | 5.2% |

  (zero-error row = the "rule of three" ≈ 3/n.)

### Inferences
- "50 per category" is a reasonable *screen* (it catches a labeller that is badly wrong) but it cannot certify a label error rate low enough not to distort a 95% recall estimate: 1 error in 50 is consistent with up to ~9% error, and section 5 shows 5% gold false positives already pull a true 0.95 recall down to ~0.90.
- Spot-check the two error directions separately per cue class: (a) a sample of LLM *positives* (estimates gold precision, i.e. how many gold positives are wrong), and (b) a sample of LLM *negatives* enriched toward near-misses (gate fired, LLM said no; or the second LLM disagreed) to find implicit cues the LLM missed. Report ppos/pneg or precision/recall of the LLM vs human, not just kappa.
- Thresholds: the commonly quoted Landis & Koch bands (0.61–0.80 "substantial", >0.80 "almost perfect") are conventions and are distorted by prevalence; for a recall gold set the operative threshold is the bounded error rate in the positive class, not a kappa band. (Landis & Koch bands are from background knowledge; not sourced this session.)

### Gaps
- No source found giving an accepted kappa/AC1 threshold specifically for LLM-vs-human gold-set acceptance.

## 3. Sampling rare positives: random vs stratified vs enriched, bias correction, and how many positives for recall ≈ 0.95

### Takeaway
Recall is a proportion over *positives*, so its precision depends on the number of gold positives, not the total sample. For rare cues, enrich (stratify) the sample so you get enough positives, then re-weight by stratum inclusion probabilities; an enriched sample selected using the gate's own output (or a correlated signal) is biased unless re-weighted, and must include strata where the gate did not fire. Estimating recall 0.95 to ±3 points (95% CI) needs ~215 positives *per cue class you want a separate number for*; certifying "recall ≥ 0.95" needs far more, because the lower confidence bound must clear 0.95.

### Cited Findings
- Technology-assisted review (e-discovery) literature treats recall estimation from samples as a statistical certification problem: Lewis, Yang & Frieder (CIKM 2021) give the first broadly applicable statistically valid sample-based stopping rules for one-phase TAR using quantile estimation, and show that *overshooting* a recall target to be safe is a major source of excess cost, so a larger validation sample often lowers total cost — [arXiv 2108.12746](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.12746.pdf); [DeepAI summary](https://deepai.org/publication/certifying-one-phase-technology-assisted-reviews)
- Confidence-driven inference (Gligorić et al. 2024): combine LLM labels with LLM confidence to choose which items get human labels; yields provably valid confidence intervals "no less accurate than if we only relied on human annotations", and cut required human annotations by >25% on politeness, stance and bias tasks — [arXiv 2408.15204](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15204)
- Computed: two-sided 95% intervals when the gate finds 95% of n gold positives:

  | gold positives n | hits | Wilson 95% CI | half-width | Clopper-Pearson 95% CI | one-sided 95% lower bound (CP) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | 50 | 48 | 0.865 – 0.989 | ±6.2 pts | 0.863 – 0.995 | 0.879 |
  | 100 | 95 | 0.888 – 0.978 | ±4.5 pts | 0.887 – 0.984 | 0.898 |
  | 200 | 190 | 0.910 – 0.973 | ±3.1 pts | 0.910 – 0.976 | 0.917 |
  | 400 | 380 | 0.924 – 0.967 | ±2.2 pts | 0.924 – 0.969 | 0.928 |

- Computed: positives needed for a Wilson half-width at p = 0.95: ±5 pts → 83; ±3 pts → 215; ±2 pts → 469 (normal approximation gives 203 / 457; ±1 pt → ~1,825).
- Computed: to *certify* recall ≥ 0.95 (one-sided 95% Clopper-Pearson lower bound ≥ 0.95), the maximum number of misses allowed is: n=50 → impossible even with 0 misses (LB 0.942); n=59 → 0 misses; n=100 → 1 miss; n=200 → 4; n=400 → 12; n=600 → 20; n=1000 → 38. For a ≥ 0.90 bar: n=50 → 1 miss, 100 → 4, 200 → 12, 400 → 29.
- Computed: probability of being able to certify LB ≥ 0.95 (power) if the gate's true recall is 0.97: n=100 → 20%, 200 → 28%, 400 → 58%, 600 → 73%, 1000 → 94%. If true recall is 0.98: n=100 → 40%, 200 → 63%, 400 → 94%, 600 → 99%.

### Inferences
- Stratified/enriched estimator (standard survey-sampling form): with strata h of size N_h in the population (e.g. sentences by cue-lexicon hit vs no hit, by source, by LLM-confidence band), sampled at rate π_h, each sampled gold positive gets weight 1/π_h, and recall = Σ w_i·[gate fired on i]·[gold positive] / Σ w_i·[gold positive]. Use a bootstrap over strata (or a stratified variance formula) for the CI; the effective number of positives is lower than the raw count when weights are unequal.
- Enrichment that uses the gate itself (e.g. sample mostly sentences where the gate fired) is the classic way to inflate recall: the missed positives live in the gate-negative stratum, which is exactly the low-prevalence stratum, so it must be sampled explicitly (enriched by a *different* signal: the LLM label, a second LLM, a broader keyword list, or plain random) and up-weighted. A "found by the gate's own lexicon" positive set measures nothing about the lexicon's coverage.
- Practical sizing for five cue classes: ±3 pts per class needs ~215 positives each (~1,075 positives total). If only a pooled number is needed, 200–400 positives total suffice for ±3/±2 pts, but per-class recall then stays unknown, and a rare class can sit well below 0.95 while the pooled figure looks fine.
- A "95% recall target" should be stated as a decision rule up front: either "point estimate ≥ 0.95 with the CI reported" (achievable at 200–400 positives) or "lower bound ≥ 0.95" (needs ~400–1,000 positives and a gate whose true recall is ≥ 0.97–0.98). The second is much stricter; with 100 positives a gate that is truly at 0.97 fails certification 80% of the time.

### Gaps
- No paper found that sizes gold sets specifically for linguistic cue-recall audits; the sizing above is generic binomial reasoning plus the TAR literature.
- Importance-weighted recall estimators for classifier evaluation (e.g. Bennett & Carvalho; Sawade et al. active estimation of F-measures) were not retrieved this session.

## 4. Avoiding overfitting the test set: held-out ownership, leakage, adaptive overfitting, frozen benchmarks, refresh, and contamination when the same LLM labels and the developer sees errors

### Takeaway
Repeatedly checking a gate against the same held-out set and fixing what it misses turns the held-out set into training data (adaptive overfitting). The defences: split a *development* set the developer may inspect freely from a *frozen test* set the developer never sees item-by-item; give the test set to a different owner (QA) who reports only aggregate numbers (or pass/fail, Thresholdout-style); version and hash it; and refresh it with newly drawn data on a schedule, since freshly drawn data is the real check.

### Cited Findings
- Dwork, Feldman, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold & Roth (Science 2015, "The reusable holdout"): standard validity guarantees assume a procedure fixed before seeing the data, but real analysis is adaptive; their Thresholdout mechanism lets an analyst reuse a holdout set for many adaptively chosen checks by revealing holdout information only when the holdout result differs from the training result by more than a threshold (plus noise), drawing on differential privacy — [arXiv 1506.02629](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.02629.pdf); [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/the-reusable-holdout-preserving-validity-in-adaptive-data-analysis/); [Roth's page](https://cis.upenn.edu/~aaroth/reusable.html)
- Recht, Roelofs, Schmidt & Shankar (ICML 2019): new test sets built by re-running the CIFAR-10 / ImageNet collection process showed accuracy drops of 3–15% (CIFAR-10) and 11–14% (ImageNet); the authors attribute the drop mainly to distribution shift toward slightly "harder" items rather than to adaptive reuse of the old test set, and gains on the old test set still carried over — [PMLR v97](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/recht19a)
- Pangakis et al. recommend validating LLM labels against human labels as part of the workflow, i.e. the human set is the reference, not the LLM output — [arXiv 2306.00176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00176)
- Stolwijk et al.: choosing a model by F1 on the validation labels increased downstream bias — an example of selection on a validation metric leaking into conclusions — [arXiv 2512.08404](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08404v1)

### Inferences
- Recommended governance for a cue gate (inference from the above, adapted to a dev/QA split):
  - **Two splits, drawn at random from the stratified pool before anyone looks**: a dev split (the developer reads misses and extends the lexicon from it) and a test split (frozen; sha256 of the file recorded in the code/sidecar; QA-owned).
  - **The developer never reads test-split items or its per-item misses.** QA reports recall per cue class with CI and, at most, miss *counts* by class; per-item misses go to QA only. Each time a test-split miss is shown to the developer, that item moves to dev and the test split shrinks.
  - **Limit test evaluations**: one scored evaluation per release / per ticket, logged; Thresholdout-style "pass/fail only" reporting is the formal version of this.
  - **Refresh**: a new test split drawn from articles newer than the freeze date (e.g. quarterly, or when new sources/topics enter the corpus), labelled with the *same frozen prompt and model*. Recht et al. show the new-sample check is what catches distribution shift; the old split catches regressions.
  - **Contamination specific to LLM-labelled sets**: if the gate's lexicon was built by reading LLM rationales or LLM-proposed cue words, and the same LLM labelled the test set, the gate and the gold set share the LLM's blind spots (implicit cues both miss) and measured recall is inflated. Mitigations: label the test split with a different model family than any model used to draft the lexicon, or human-label the test split's disagreement/near-miss strata; keep the labelling prompt free of the gate's word list.
- Do not tune the gate's lexicon against the human-verified items either: they are the most valuable part of the test split.

### Gaps
- No published governance standard specific to dev/QA split ownership for rule lexicons was found; the procedure above is synthesised from the reusable-holdout and benchmark-replication literature.

## 5. Label-noise effects on measured recall, and using two LLMs / self-consistency to flag uncertain labels for human review

### Takeaway
Gold-label errors bias measured recall in both directions: false gold positives (LLM over-labelling) push measured recall *down*; missed implicit cues (LLM under-labelling) push it *up* by removing the hard cases. At a 0.95 target, even 5% false positives in the gold set moves a true 0.95 to a measured ~0.90, which is as large as the entire CI at 200 positives. Disagreement between two different LLMs, or low LLM confidence, is the efficient way to route items to human review, and confidence-driven inference shows this can keep CIs valid while cutting human labels by >25%.

### Cited Findings
- Confidence-driven inference uses LLM confidence to decide which items humans annotate, with guaranteed-valid confidence intervals and >25% fewer human labels across three tasks — [arXiv 2408.15204](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15204)
- LLM-vs-human disagreement is systematic: only ~20% of items got identical labels from all 20 LLMs and the manual coders, and models differ in prevalence bias — [arXiv 2512.08404](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08404v1); this implies different LLMs fail on different items, which is what makes cross-model disagreement a useful review flag.
- GPT-4 judgement alignment with humans on MAVEN event annotations was strongly correlated with the certainty level of the model's own response (five Rubin-style certainty levels) — [Assessing Crowdsourced Annotations with LLMs, NLP4DH 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.nlp4dh-1.16)
- Gilardi et al. report ChatGPT's run-to-run agreement exceeded crowd workers and trained annotators — [arXiv 2303.15056](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15056); high self-consistency is therefore not evidence of correctness, only of stability.
- Computed: measured recall = (1 − e)·R + e·f, where R is the gate's true recall, e the share of gold positives that are actually negative, and f the gate's firing rate on those non-cue items. With R = 0.95:

  | gold false-positive share e | f = 0.02 | f = 0.10 |
  |---|---|---|
  | 2% | 0.931 | 0.933 |
  | 5% | 0.903 | 0.907 |
  | 10% | 0.857 | 0.865 |

  (Gold false negatives do not enter this formula; they bias recall upward only if the missed positives are harder than average for the gate, which is the expected case for implicit cues.)

### Inferences
- Review routing (in order of yield): (1) items where two LLMs from different families disagree; (2) items where the gate and the LLM disagree in either direction (gate fired / LLM no; LLM yes / gate silent — the latter are the candidate misses that define recall); (3) low-confidence or self-inconsistent LLM labels (vary sampling seed or paraphrase the prompt; items whose label flips are uncertain); (4) a small uniform random slice from the agree-agree stratum to estimate the residual error the routing does not catch. Without (4) there is no estimate of the error in the unreviewed bulk.
- Self-consistency of one model catches stochastic uncertainty but not systematic blind spots; cross-family disagreement is the stronger flag for implicit Indonesian cues.
- Every human correction should be recorded alongside the LLM label (not overwrite it), so the LLM's per-class error rate on the test split is itself measurable and reportable with the CI from section 2.
- Since gold false positives bias recall downward by roughly e·(R − f), the gold set's positive-class precision should be verified to ≥ ~98% (bounded, e.g. 0 errors in ~150 human-checked positives gives a 2% one-sided upper bound per the rule of three) before a measured 0.93–0.95 recall is interpreted as a gate shortfall rather than label noise.

### Gaps
- No study found quantifying how much two-LLM disagreement routing reduces residual label error specifically for discourse cues or for Indonesian.
- No direct measurement found of LLMs' miss rate on implicit (connective-free) causal or hedge cues in Indonesian.
