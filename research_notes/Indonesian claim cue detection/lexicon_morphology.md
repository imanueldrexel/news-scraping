# Lexicon + morphology detection of discourse/claim cues in Indonesian news

Scope: how far a deterministic lexicon (roots expanded with Indonesian affixes) can go in detecting five cue types (causal, hedge/speculation, denial/negation, temporal end point, future/plan) in Indonesian news sentences. Researched 2026-10-21. Numbers from non-Indonesian benchmarks are labelled with their language/domain.

Source-handling note: several WebFetch summaries of PDFs were unreliable (one invented "220 hedge cues", "61% lexicon recall" and "34% ambiguous cues" for CoNLL-2010 that are not in the paper). Every number below from an ACL/arXiv paper was checked against the PDF's extracted text, not a summary.

## Q1. Indonesian morphological analysers and stemmers: which exist, which GENERATE affixed forms, how accurate, and the known pitfalls

### Takeaway
There are four usable tools. Sastrawi is a stemmer (analysis only, ~95% of affixed words stemmed correctly). MorphInd is a Foma finite-state analyser (2011; 84.6% coverage, and its FST could in principle run backwards). Aksara is a UD v2 analyser/lemmatiser (94.83% lemma accuracy). IndoMorph (2025) is the only one built and published as a word GENERATOR. It finds a correct form for 97.75% of KBBI derived words, but only 32% of the forms it generates are right. So generating forms from roots gives high recall at low precision. To be safe, the generated forms have to be filtered against a word list or corpus, or hand-checked. And news text contains non-standard affixed spellings (e.g. *mempengaruhi* next to standard *memengaruhi*) that a rule generator following the standard will not produce.

### Cited Findings
- **Sastrawi** implements Nazief & Adriani, Asian (2007), Arifin et al. (2009) Enhanced Confix Stripping, and Tahitoe & Purwitasari (2010) modified ECS. It uses a root dictionary taken from kateglo.com "dengan sedikit perubahan". It only stems (e.g. "menahan => tahan"), has no generation, and the repo is marked [Inactive]. — [sastrawi/sastrawi GitHub](https://github.com/sastrawi/sastrawi)
- Stemmer accuracy on 900 affixed words, checked against KBBI: Sastrawi 95.2%, Nazief & Adriani 92.4%, Arifin-Setiono 89% (Mustikasari et al., BIS-STE 2020, publ. 2021). — [UMPO eprints](https://eprints.umpo.ac.id/12871/)
- Asian, Williams & Tahaghoghi (ACSC 2005): given a reasonable dictionary, Nazief & Adriani stems ~93% of word occurrences correctly, and their improvements bring it to almost 95%. The confix-stripping journal version followed in 2007 (ACM TALIP). — [search summary of CRPIT abstract](https://crpit.scem.westernsydney.edu.au/abstracts/CRPITV38Asian.html); [UI Scholar entry](https://scholar.ui.ac.id/en/publications/stemming-indonesian-a-confix-stripping-approach/) (abstract page itself unreachable: DNS failure)
- **MorphInd** (Larasati, Kuboň, Zeman 2011) is a finite-state, two-level-morphology tool built in Foma that does analysis and lemmatisation, with a more detailed tagset than earlier analysers. It reached 84.6% coverage on an Indonesian corpus and mostly failed on proper nouns and foreign words. — [Springer chapter page](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/indonesian-morphology-tool-morphind-towards-an-indonesian-corpus/3708380)
- MorphInd cannot handle reduplication: *es krim-es krim* is analysed as three words. — [IndoMorph, SEALP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf)
- Earlier FST work: Pisceldo et al. (2008) built a two-level FST network covering affixes and reduplication, but not every affix/clitic combination. — [IndoMorph, SEALP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf); original [ALTA 2008, U08-1018](https://aclanthology.org/U08-1018/)
- MALINDO Morph (Nomoto et al. 2018) is a morphological dictionary/analyser for Malay and Indonesian. The IndoMorph authors report it contains words that exist in neither language and has inconsistent analyses/rules. — [IndoMorph, SEALP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf)
- **Aksara** (Universitas Indonesia, UD v2) does word segmentation (96.9% accuracy), case-sensitive lemmatisation (94.83%), POS tagging (88.2 F1) and morphological features (9 of 18 feature tags above 80 F1). It was evaluated on a UD v2 Indonesian treebank and is reported to outperform MorphInd. — [aksara on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/aksara/); [GitHub ir-nlp-csui/aksara](https://github.com/ir-nlp-csui/aksara)
- **IndoMorph** (Kamajaya & Moeljadi, SEALP 2025) is described as "a morphology analyzer and word generator". Its forward generator takes a cluster (affix group) and a base form and outputs all possible derived words, applying per-rule exception lists ("Kata Dikecualikan"). It covers all prefix/suffix/circumfix/clitic/particle combinations (e.g. *ku-+meN-*, *-kan+-mu*, *-an+-nya+-lah*) plus reduplication; partial and imitative reduplication are listed because they are not productive. The dataset is public (xlsx on GitHub). — [IndoMorph, SEALP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf)
- IndoMorph evaluation: for 27,106 derived words in KBBI (April 2023), it produced at least one correct formation for **97.75% (26,496)**. But of the **83,597 formations generated, only 26,803 (32.06%) were accurate**, and the authors call it "comprehensive in morphology generation but low in morphology precision". Systematic *ber-* errors were fixed later, but the human-checked test was not repeated after the fix. — [IndoMorph, SEALP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf)
- Morphophonemics of the nasal prefix as IndoMorph states it: with meN-/peN-, a base loses its initial consonant if it is voiceless p, t, s or k and keeps it otherwise; with a one-syllable base, meN- becomes *menge-* (peN- → *penge-*). — [IndoMorph, SEALP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf)
- Non-standard forms are frequent in real writing, "especially regarding the morphophonemic rules, e.g. *mempengaruhi* and *memengaruhi*, *berterbangan* and *beterbangan*, *mengedip-kedipkan* and *mengedip-ngedipkan*". IndoMorph suggests deliberately writing error rules to cover them. — [IndoMorph, SEALP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf)
- Affix inventory: prefixes meN-, di-, ber-, ter-, peN-, per-, ke-, se-; suffixes -kan, -i, -an; circumfixes ke-…-an, peN-…-an, per-…-an, se-…-nya; proclitics ku-/kau-; enclitics -ku/-mu/-nya; particles -lah/-kah. Infixes are fossilised and not productive. — [IndoMorph, SEALP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf)
- Four types of reduplication (full, partial, imitative, affixed); affixed reduplication can sit on the left (*mencium-cium*), the right (*cium-mencium*) or both sides (*seberhasil-berhasilnya*). — [IndoMorph, SEALP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf)
- Temporal-domain morphology is affixed too: *setahun* (se- = one), *sepagian*, and the plural duration *berjam-jam* (ber- + reduplication). IndoTimex had to strip affixes before parsing. — [Mirza PhD thesis 2016, §10.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.08120)

### Inferences
- If the goal is "expand roots into every affixed surface form", IndoMorph is the closest off-the-shelf generator and MorphInd's Foma FST is the second option (in principle FSTs run both ways; I found no published evaluation of MorphInd used as a generator). Sastrawi and Aksara can only check a generated list after the fact (stem/lemmatise each corpus token and compare to the root), not produce it.
- IndoMorph's 97.75%/32% split shows what blind affix expansion does: for cue detection, roughly two out of three generated strings would be non-words. That costs little when the strings are only matched against text (a non-word never appears), but it matters when the expansion creates a **real word with another meaning** (e.g. root *tekan* → *tekanan* 'pressure (noun)', *tertekan* 'under pressure/depressed', *penekanan* 'emphasis'). That is a precision problem the morphology cannot see.
- Rather than generating forms, the more reliable way to build the lexicon is the reverse direction: lemmatise a large in-domain news corpus (Aksara, or Sastrawi plus a dictionary check) and collect every surface form whose root is in the cue-root list. That captures the forms actually used, including non-standard ones like *mempengaruhi*, *mensukseskan*, and unassimilated loanwords (*mengkritik*, *memproduksi*), which a standard-rule generator will miss or get wrong.
- Pitfalls to plan for (from general Indonesian grammar knowledge; not found in a single source with numbers): exceptions to nasal assimilation (*mempunyai* keeps p; loanword roots and consonant clusters keep their initial: *memprotes*, *mentransfer*); ambiguous back-derivations from meN- forms (*memukul* ← *pukul*, but *menyanyi* ← *nyanyi*; *mengubah* ← *ubah*), which is why IndoMorph's analyser returns several candidates (*mukul*/*pukul*); and ter- meaning accidental, superlative, stative or abilitive, so *ter-* + root is not one meaning.
- Over-stemming hits cue detection directly: a stemmer reducing *berakhir* → *akhir* and *akhirnya* → *akhir* merges an end-point verb with a discourse adverb ('finally'). Match surface forms or root+affix pairs, not bare stems.

### Gaps
- No independent head-to-head evaluation of IndoMorph vs MorphInd vs Aksara on generation was found; IndoMorph's authors say they "cannot be directly compared".
- No published numbers on how often non-standard morphophonemic spellings appear in Indonesian online news (vs essays). IndoMorph only says they are "frequent" in essays.
- The Paperity comparative stemmer evaluation (Sastrawi vs ECS etc.) returned 403; its numbers were not checked.
- No source checked for MorphInd's lemmatisation accuracy beyond the 84.6% coverage figure.

## Q2. Published Indonesian lexicons/corpora of discourse connectives, causal connectives, hedge cues, negation cues and temporal expressions

### Takeaway
For Indonesian, only temporal expressions have a real, evaluated rule-based resource: IndoTimex (Mirza 2015/2016), 92.87 F1 recognition on news. I found no Indonesian PDTB-style discourse treebank, no Indonesian hedge-cue or negation-cue corpus in the CoNLL-2010 / *SEM 2012 style, and no Indonesian causal-relation extraction dataset. The Indonesian resources that touch causality (XCOPA-id, COPAL-ID, IndoNLI, SNLI-Indo) are reasoning/NLI benchmarks with no cue annotations. The cue lexicons will have to be curated in-house, using linguistics papers and existing English cue inventories as seeds.

### Cited Findings
- **Temporal (Indonesian):** IndoTimex is a rule-based recogniser built as a finite-state transducer derived from a TimeNorm synchronous CFG translated to Indonesian. On 25 news articles (9,549 tokens) it reaches **92.87% F1 for recognising temporal expressions and 85.26% F1 for normalising them**. Code: github.com/paramitamirza/IndoTimex. — [Mirza PhD thesis 2016, §10.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.08120)
- Mirza notes the only earlier Indonesian timex work (Simamora 2013) covered only DATE expressions and was not in TimeML, and argues that low-resource languages like Indonesian still need manual extension despite HeidelTime's automatic 200+-language resources. — [Mirza PhD thesis 2016, §10.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.08120)
- Indonesian-specific temporal issues Mirza lists: day-month-year order, "05.30" with a dot, decimal comma ("2,5 jam"), Roman-numeral centuries ("abad XXI"), *malam Minggu* = Saturday night, only two seasons, affixed durations (*setahun*, *berjam-jam*), and the fact that Indonesian verbs don't mark tense, so aspect markers (*sudah*, *sedang*, *akan*) or temporal expressions carry the time. — [Mirza PhD thesis 2016, §10.5.1–10.5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.08120)
- A separate rule-based Indonesian temporal-entity tagger (IPB repository): 40 rules derived from 100 documents, 100% accuracy on those 100 documents and 94.57% on 34 test documents. — [IPB repository (search summary)](https://repository.ipb.ac.id/handle/123456789/64661) (only seen as a search snippet; record not opened)
- HeidelTime has manual resources for 13 languages (not Indonesian) and automatically created resources for 200+ languages since v2.0, which the authors say are of "lower quality" and meant as a baseline. — [HeidelTime GitHub / Heidelberg DBS page](https://ds.ifi.uni-heidelberg.de/resources/temporal-tagging/)
- **Causal (Indonesian linguistics):** *karena* and *sebab* are the most used causal conjunctions in spoken and written discourse, and both are also lexical words meaning 'cause/reason', so the connective and noun readings overlap. — [HSE publication (search snippet)](https://publications.hse.ru/pubs/share/direct/399536499.pdf) (not opened; author/year unverified)
- Indonesian causal-reasoning datasets exist but carry no cue annotations: XCOPA (11 languages incl. Indonesian, causal commonsense), COPAL-ID (2023, Indonesian culture-grounded COPA), SNLI-Indo (RTE). NusaCrowd (2022) catalogues Indonesian datasets. — [COPAL-ID arXiv 2311.01012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.01012); [SNLI Indo, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10792674/); [NusaCrowd arXiv 2212.09648](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.09648)
- **Discourse connectives:** a search for an Indonesian PDTB-style corpus turned up PDTB-style treebanks for Chinese, Czech, Turkish and Hindi but none for Indonesian. — [search results: CUHK Chinese DTB](https://preview.aclanthology.org/menus/L14-1246/), [PDTB overview, LDC](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/docs/LDC2008T05/papers/frontiers04.pdf). Indonesian discourse-marker work found is corpus linguistics on EFL writing or colloquial particles (*kok*, *ya*, *nggak*), not news connectives. — [UINSA thesis](https://digilib.uinsa.ac.id/id/eprint/79809/), [kok in Jakartan Indonesian](https://e-journal.iainsalatiga.ac.id/index.php/jopr/article/view/2524)
- **Hedges (Indonesian):** only applied-linguistics corpus studies of academic writing. One counts 103.77 hedges per 10,000 words in 400 Indonesian soft-science articles (2.5M words; Cogent Arts & Humanities, Dec 2023). Another finds Indonesian authors used 365 hedges vs 859 for native English authors in ~100k words, favouring approximators. — [DOAJ, Cogent A&H 2023](https://doaj.org/article/0fd484abe2834d768ab086f6573765f9); [IDEAS journal, IAIN Palopo](https://ejournal.iainpalopo.ac.id/index.php/ideas/article/view/8315)
- **Negation (Indonesian):** found a contrastive linguistics study of German vs Indonesian negation and sentiment-analysis papers that handle negation (e.g. attention BiLSTM), but no cue-annotated corpus. — [IJAL German/Indonesian negation](https://ejournal.upi.edu/index.php/IJAL/article/download/23218/11496); [UNEJ BiLSTM negation](https://oailib.unej.ac.id/vufind/Record/IJCSS_60733). A 2021 survey of negation/speculation corpora notes that non-English coverage is still limited. — [Negation and Speculation in NLP survey, BCU](https://www.open-access.bcu.ac.uk/13504/1/Negation%20and%20Speculation%20in%20NLP%20A%20Survey%2C%20Corpora%2C%20Methods%2C%20and%20Applications.pdf)
- IndoMorph has a "Negasi" rule type for derived words containing a negation word as a morpheme (e.g. *ke- + tidak + mampu + -an* → *ketidakmampuan*). So affixal or embedded negation exists in Indonesian too, analogous to English un-/-less in *SEM 2012. — [IndoMorph, SEALP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf)

### Inferences
- Of the five cue types, only temporal end points / dates have an Indonesian tool to reuse (IndoTimex's grammar). Even it targets TIMEX3 values (dates, durations), not end-point *relations* (*hingga*, *sampai*, *paling lambat*, *berakhir pada*), which are TimeML SIGNALs/TLINK cues. The end-point connective layer has to be built in-house.
- English cue inventories can serve as translation seeds: the PDTB's 100 explicit connectives (28 causal), the Causal-TimeBank CSIGNAL/causative-verb tables, the CoNLL-2010 BioScope/Wikipedia weasel cues, and NegEx's trigger list. They are not substitutes: Indonesian expresses many of these cues through di-/ter- passives and verb derivation (*dipicu*, *terseret*, *dikabarkan*, *diperkirakan*) rather than separate function words.
- Since no Indonesian gold set exists for any of the five cue types, a recall claim (e.g. "95%") can only be measured against a sample annotated in-house. Nothing published supports or refutes it for Indonesian.

### Gaps
- Did not find (and do not believe there is) an Indonesian PDTB/RST discourse treebank. DISRPT shared-task language lists were not checked for Indonesian.
- No Indonesian hedge- or negation-cue annotated corpus found; the UI/ITB thesis literature on "deteksi negasi"/"kalimat spekulatif" was not searched in Indonesian.
- Did not read Mirza (2015) for IndoTimex's error analysis; only the thesis summary numbers.
- The IPB tagger and the HSE karena/sebab paper were seen only as snippets.

## Q3. Recall of lexicon/cue-list approaches on the benchmark tasks (other languages, labelled), and the share of causal relations that are explicitly cued

### Takeaway
On English benchmarks, cue detection is the easy part when cues form a closed class. Negation cues reach ~92 F1 (*SEM 2012, fiction) and explicit discourse-connective identification ~94–95 (PDTB, news, with syntax). Hedge cues are harder: 81 F1 in biomedical text, but only 36.5 F1 for Wikipedia "weasel" cues, whose forms are open-ended. A pure trigger list for negation (NegEx) gets 77.8% sensitivity in clinical text. Rule-based temporal taggers reach ~90–93 F1 extraction with hand-built rules (HeidelTime, IndoTimex) and ~65–73 F1 with auto-translated rules. Most causality is NOT explicitly cued: in the PDTB, only 46.75% of Contingency relations are explicit, and only 12% of explicit connectives are causal. Even explicit causal relation extraction (finding the link plus its arguments) with rules has low recall (~16%).

### Cited Findings
**Hedge cues — CoNLL-2010 (English; biomedical BioScope + Wikipedia weasels)**
- Task 1 sentence-level (is the sentence uncertain?): best biological F 86.4 (Tang, P 85.0 / R 87.7); best Wikipedia F 60.2 (Georgescul, P 72.0 / R 51.7). 23 teams took part. — [Farkas et al. 2010, CoNLL-2010 overview](https://aclanthology.org/W10-3001.pdf)
- **Cue-level** (strict match of the cue phrase): best biological F 81.3 (Tang, P 81.7 / R 81.0); the lowest listed system had F 53.1. Best **Wikipedia cue-level F 36.5 (P 63.0 / R 25.7)**, with others at 33.7, 19.5 and 11.3. — [Farkas et al. 2010](https://aclanthology.org/W10-3001.pdf)
- The organisers explain: "biological sentences have relatively simple patterns… while Wikipedia weasels have a diverse nature". Most systems had higher precision than recall, possibly because "patterns occurring in the evaluation set may be imperfectly covered by the training datasets". — [Farkas et al. 2010](https://aclanthology.org/W10-3001.pdf)
- A **lexical baseline** (Tjong Kim Sang 2010) weights each cue word or bigram by how often it occurs in certain vs uncertain training sentences and scores a sentence by its best cue. Its official Task 1 scores were 80.2 (bio) and 54.4 (Wikipedia). With bigrams and a per-dataset threshold, after the deadline, it reached F 82.0 (bio, P 79.8 / R 84.4) and F 62.8 (Wikipedia, P 62.2 / R 63.5), above the official best Wikipedia score of 60.2. — [Tjong Kim Sang 2010](https://ifarm.nl/erikt/papers/conll2010.pdf); [official table in Farkas et al.](https://aclanthology.org/W10-3001.pdf)

**Negation cues — *SEM 2012 (English; Conan Doyle stories, CD-SCO)**
- Cues can be single words (*never*), multiwords (*no longer*, *by no means*) or affixes (*im-*, *-less*). — [Morante & Blanco 2012](https://aclanthology.org/S12-1035.pdf)
- Best cue detection F1 was 92.34 (FBK, closed track; CRFs plus a special procedure for sub-token/affixal cues) and 91.31 (UiO2, open track). Scope resolution is much lower (72.40 best), as is negated-event detection (67.02). The organisers attribute high cue scores to the fact that "the set of negation cues is closed and comprises mostly single tokens". — [Morante & Blanco 2012](https://aclanthology.org/S12-1035.pdf)
- Rule-based systems (UCM-1 used "a predefined list of cues" plus WordNet for affixal cues) scored clearly lower on the global task (best rule-based global F1 39.56 vs 54.82 for ML in the open track). The per-cue-column values could not be reliably read from the extracted table. — [Morante & Blanco 2012](https://aclanthology.org/S12-1035.pdf)
- **NegEx** (Chapman et al. 2001, English clinical discharge summaries; regex trigger-phrase list plus pseudo-negation filter and scope limit): on 1,235 findings in 1,000 sentences, sensitivity (recall) **77.8%**, specificity 94.5%, PPV 84.5%. The baseline had 88.3% recall but 68.4% PPV. — [Chapman et al. 2001 PDF (Pitt DBMI)](https://www.dbmi.pitt.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/A-simple-algorithm-for-identifying-negated-findings-and-diseases-in-discharge-summaries.pdf) (figures as given in the search summary of the abstract)

**Explicit discourse connectives — PDTB (English; WSJ news)**
- The PDTB 2.0 has 100 explicit connective types and 18,459 explicit relations (19,458 counting double senses), plus 16,584 implicit relations. — [Pitler et al. 2008, "Easily Identifiable Discourse Relations"](https://aclanthology.org/C08-2022.pdf)
- Ambiguity between discourse and non-discourse use: only 11 of the 100 connectives are used as connectives more than 90% of the time. *although* is a connective 91.4% of the time, *or* only 2.8%. — [Pitler & Nenkova 2009](https://aclanthology.org/P09-2004.pdf)
- Discourse vs non-discourse classification: connective string alone gives F 75.33 / acc 85.86; syntax alone F 88.19 / acc 92.25; connective plus syntax F 92.28 / acc 95.04, and with interaction features F 94.19. Gold parses were used, so automatic parses would score somewhat lower. — [Pitler & Nenkova 2009](https://aclanthology.org/P09-2004.pdf)
- Sense classification of explicit connectives: 93.67% accuracy from the connective alone and 94.15% with syntax, near the inter-annotator agreement of 94. *since* is causal only 52.17% of the time (otherwise temporal); *because* is 100% Contingency. — [Pitler & Nenkova 2009](https://aclanthology.org/P09-2004.pdf); [Pitler et al. 2008](https://aclanthology.org/C08-2022.pdf)

**How much causality is explicit (English news)**
- PDTB 2.0 explicit vs implicit per class: **Contingency (causal + conditional) 3,741 explicit (46.75%) vs 4,261 implicit (53.25%)**; Temporal 79.55% explicit; Comparison 69.05%; Expansion 42.04%. — [Pitler et al. 2008](https://aclanthology.org/C08-2022.pdf)
- "Over 12% of explicit discourse connectives are marked as causal, as are nearly 26% of implicit discourse relationships" in the PDTB. — [Dunietz, Levin & Carbonell 2017, BECauSE 2.0](https://aclanthology.org/W17-0812.pdf)
- Of the 102 PDTB explicit markers, 28 mark causal relations. The PDTB also annotates AltLex, an open class of alternative causal expressions (e.g. "This may help explain why…") that a closed list does not cover. — [Hidey & McKeown 2016, ACL](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1135.pdf)
- BECauSE 2.0 (English news and Congress text, construction-grammar cue annotation of verbs, adverbs, conjunctions, prepositions and complex constructions): 1,803 causal-language instances in 5,380 sentences. Nearly 45% are expressed as MOTIVATION or PURPOSE, over 14% as hypotheticals, and over a third of the annotated temporal relations are also causal. — [BECauSE 2.0](https://aclanthology.org/W17-0812.pdf)
- **Causal-TimeBank** (English TimeBank news), which annotates only *explicit* causal constructions within a sentence ("causal relations are often not overtly expressed in text"): 318 CLINKs and 171 CSIGNALs. 180 CLINKs are cued by causal signals, 115 by causative verbs, and ~23 by other context-dependent words (e.g. "means that", "essential to keeping"). Only ~60% of TimeBank documents contain any explicit causal link. Variants such as *due mostly to*, *thanks in part to*, *the main reason for* need to be handled. Prepositions (*by, from, for, with*), conjunctions (*and, as, since*) and *so* are "highly ambiguous". Some causative verbs (*cause, trigger, prevent*) carry causation on their own, while others (*make, allow, help, keep*) need a periphrastic construction. *follow* and *reflect* are ambiguous between causal and non-causal readings. — [Mirza PhD thesis 2016, §5.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.08120)
- A rule-based causal-verb module (relation extraction: event pair plus direction) reached P 0.82 / **R 0.157**; the full hybrid system reached P 0.69 / R 0.31 / F1 0.43 on Causal-TimeBank. — [Mirza PhD thesis 2016, Table 6.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.08120)

**Temporal expressions — HeidelTime / TempEval (several languages)**
- Manually built HeidelTime 1.9 rules for English: relaxed-extraction F1 90.7–91.9 and value-normalisation F1 78.1–83.1. The automatically created resources (Wiktionary translations of simplified English rules) give only 64.9–73.2 extraction F1 and 53.4–54.7 normalisation F1. Precision stays high (93–99) while recall falls (49–59). — [Strötgen & Gertz 2015, EMNLP](https://aclanthology.org/D15-1063.pdf); [HeidelTime GitHub](https://github.com/HeidelTime/heideltime)
- Manual HeidelTime results reported across 11 other languages fall roughly in the 78–94 relaxed-extraction F1 range (e.g. Spanish TempEval-3 news 93.8; the table rows were partly garbled in extraction, so these are per-language values only approximately). — [Strötgen & Gertz 2015, Table 1](https://aclanthology.org/D15-1063.pdf)
- Indonesian (IndoTimex, hand-built rules, news): 92.87 F1 recognition, 85.26 F1 normalisation. — [Mirza 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.08120)

### Inferences
- The benchmarks split along one line: **closed-class, mostly single-token cues** (negation particles, PDTB connectives, calendar words) reach about 90–95 F1 with lexicons plus light disambiguation, while **open-class, paraphrastic cues** (Wikipedia weasels, AltLex causal phrases, causative verbs needing a construction) stay far lower (36.5 cue F1 on weasels; 15.7% recall for rule-based causal verbs).
- Hand-built lexicons beat translated ones by a wide margin (HeidelTime manual ~91 vs auto ~65–73 extraction F1, with recall carrying the loss). Translating an English cue list into Indonesian should therefore be expected to lose recall unless it is grown from Indonesian corpus examples.
- The recall loss lies almost entirely in recall, not precision (HeidelTime auto P 93–99, R 49–59; CoNLL systems "higher precision than recall"; NegEx R 77.8 vs P 84.5). A curated lexicon tends to be precise and incomplete, matching the project's QA experience that closed word lists fail on the first unlisted synonym.
- For causal cues, "explicit" covers more than conjunctions: in Causal-TimeBank about 36% of explicit links (115/318) are carried by causative verbs and ~7% (23/318) by context-dependent words, so a conjunction-only list caps out near 57% (180/318) of *explicit* causal links. Indonesian news leans heavily on verb-encoded causation (*memicu/dipicu*, *menyebabkan*, *mengakibatkan*, *berdampak*, *terseret*, *terimbas*), so the verb share is probably at least as high. That last point is an inference: no Indonesian count exists.

### Gaps

## Q4. Is ~95% recall on explicit cues plausible with a curated lexicon plus morphology, and where is the ceiling?

### Takeaway
About 95% recall is plausible only for the closed-class, mostly single-token part of each cue type, and only when recall is measured against explicit, lexically marked instances in a sample annotated from the same news source. Benchmarks reach ~92 F1 for negation cues, ~94–95 for explicit-connective identification and ~91–93 F1 for hand-built temporal rules; IndoTimex gets 92.87 on Indonesian news. For open-class cues (causal verbs and phrases, weasel/attribution hedges such as *disebut-sebut*, *menurut sumber*, denial paraphrases, plan constructions) the published evidence points to recall well below 95% (hedge cue F1 36.5 on Wikipedia; 15.7% recall for rule-based causal verbs; NegEx 77.8%), unless the lexicon is grown iteratively from in-domain misses. A hard ceiling lies above all of this: implicit causality (53% of PDTB Contingency relations, and only ~60% of news documents have any explicit causal link) cannot be reached by any lexicon. The second ceiling is ambiguity, which costs precision, not recall. Examples: *karena*/*sebab* as noun vs connective, *since*-style causal/temporal words (52% causal), and *tekanan*/*tertekan* as noun/state vs causal verb.

### Cited Findings
- The closed-class ceiling: negation cue F1 92.34 / 91.31 because cues are "closed and… mostly single tokens". — [Morante & Blanco 2012](https://aclanthology.org/S12-1035.pdf)
- Explicit connectives: lexical identity alone gives 93.67% sense accuracy, but discourse/non-discourse detection needs syntax (F 75.33 → 92.28–94.19). A list will recall nearly every connective string, but precision depends on disambiguation. — [Pitler & Nenkova 2009](https://aclanthology.org/P09-2004.pdf)
- Only 11 of 100 English connectives are used as connectives more than 90% of the time; *since* is causal 52.17% of the time. — [Pitler & Nenkova 2009](https://aclanthology.org/P09-2004.pdf); [Pitler et al. 2008](https://aclanthology.org/C08-2022.pdf)
- Open-class hedges: Wikipedia weasel cue-level F 36.5 (R 25.7); the organisers point to the "diverse nature" of weasels. — [Farkas et al. 2010](https://aclanthology.org/W10-3001.pdf)
- Implicit share of causality: 53.25% of PDTB Contingency is implicit; ~26% of implicit relations are causal vs ~12% of explicit connectives. — [Pitler et al. 2008](https://aclanthology.org/C08-2022.pdf); [BECauSE 2.0](https://aclanthology.org/W17-0812.pdf)
- Variants of explicit causal markers (insertions such as *due mostly to*, *thanks in part to*), ambiguous prepositions/conjunctions, and verbs that are causal only in a periphrastic construction or with an event subject: "if their subjects are not events, they cannot be considered as causal markers". — [Mirza 2016, §5.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.08120)
- Morphology recall for generated forms: 97.75% of KBBI derived words get a correct generated form from IndoMorph. That is roughly the morphological ceiling for root-plus-affix expansion done by rules, against dictionary words and before any non-standard spellings. — [IndoMorph 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf)
- Non-standard morphophonemic spellings (*mempengaruhi*) are common in written Indonesian. — [IndoMorph 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.sealp-1.7.pdf)
- Translated rules lose recall: HeidelTime auto vs manual extraction F1 64.9–73.2 vs 90.7–91.9. — [Strötgen & Gertz 2015](https://aclanthology.org/D15-1063.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Per cue type for Indonesian news** (inference, no Indonesian measurement exists):
  - *Temporal end points* (*hingga, sampai, sampai dengan, s.d., berakhir (pada), paling lambat, selambat-lambatnya, batas waktu, tenggat, per [tanggal]*): a closed class with little morphology (*berakhir*, *diakhiri*, *mengakhiri*; reduplicated *selambat-lambatnya*). About 95% recall of the cue is plausible. The difficulty is that *hingga/sampai* are also degree/extent and result connectives (*hingga* 'so that', *sampai* 'until/to the point of/reach'), which costs precision, not recall. IndoTimex's ~93 F1 on Indonesian news supports a ~90–95 ceiling for the attached date expression.
  - *Future/plan* (*akan, bakal, hendak, berencana, rencananya, dijadwalkan, dicanangkan, ditargetkan, menargetkan, diproyeksikan, siap [verb], segera*): the core aspect markers are closed class, and di-/meN- derivation of *jadwal, target, rencana, proyeksi, canang* is regular. About 95% for that part is plausible. Recall drops on periphrastic plans (*tengah menyiapkan*, *membuka peluang untuk*) and on future meaning carried only by a dated temporal expression with no marker, which Indonesian allows because verbs don't mark tense ([Mirza 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.08120)).
  - *Denials* (*membantah, bantahan, menepis, menyangkal, mengelak, tidak benar, bukan…, keliru, hoaks, menampik, membantah keras*): a small core verb set with regular meN-/di-/-an derivation. Paraphrases (*meluruskan kabar*, *menegaskan tidak pernah*, *mengklarifikasi*) are open-ended. By analogy with NegEx (77.8% recall for a trigger list) and *SEM's closed-class 92 F1, recall is probably ~90%+ for single-word denials and lower for paraphrases.
  - *Hedges/speculation* (*diduga, dugaan, terduga, diperkirakan, perkiraan, dikabarkan, kabarnya, disebut-sebut, ditengarai, disinyalir, konon, kemungkinan, berpotensi, tampaknya, sepertinya, mungkin, belum dipastikan*): the di-passive of reporting/epistemic verbs is very productive, which helps a morphology-expanded lexicon. But news attribution hedges (*menurut sumber*, *kabar yang beredar*) behave like Wikipedia weasels, where cue F1 was 36.5. 95% is plausible only if attribution weasels are excluded from scope.
  - *Causal* (*karena, sebab, akibat, lantaran, gara-gara, berkat, imbas, dampak, buntut, sehingga, maka, oleh karena itu* + verbs *menyebabkan, mengakibatkan, memicu/dipicu, mendorong/didorong, berdampak, berimbas, terimbas, terseret, tertekan*): the hardest. The conjunction core is closed class, but the verb part is open, and several verbs are causal only in some constructions (*tertekan* 'under pressure', which in market news is a state, not a cause link; *didorong* 'pushed/driven by'; *seiring* 'along with', ambiguous like English *as*). Applying the Causal-TimeBank split (57% of explicit links from signals, 36% from verbs, 7% other) means a list must cover verb constructions to get past ~60%, and the last ~7% are context-dependent words no list predicts. 95% of *explicit* causal cues is a stretch target that probably requires repeated mining of misses. 95% of *all* causal claims is out of reach, because roughly half are implicit.
- **Where the ceiling is, in order:** (1) implicit relations, which no lexicon can reach (causal especially); (2) open-class paraphrases and idioms (*buntut dari*, *tak lepas dari*, *menjadi biang*, *dipicu oleh sentimen*), each a new entry the first time it is seen; (3) non-standard morphology and loanword affixation (*mempengaruhi*, *mengkritik*), fixed by mining surface forms from the corpus rather than generating them; (4) ambiguity (noun vs verb *tekanan/tertekan*, *karena* as noun, *hingga* result vs end point), a precision problem that needs local syntax or POS (Aksara's 88.2 POS F1 caps a POS-based disambiguator), the same way Pitler & Nenkova needed syntax to go from 75 to 92–94 F1.
- A defensible engineering claim is "≥95% recall on the explicit, single-token-or-fixed-multiword cues present in a held-out annotated sample of our own news, re-measured on every lexicon change". Reporting coverage per cue type separately from implicit/paraphrastic misses stops one headline number from hiding the open-class tail.

### Gaps
- No study measures lexicon recall on Indonesian news for any of the five cue types; every Indonesian per-cue estimate above is an inference by analogy with English benchmarks.
- No source on the share of hedges, denials or plans that are implicit (vs lexically marked) in news; the explicit/implicit split was found only for causal/discourse relations (PDTB, Causal-TimeBank).
- No source quantifying how often *tertekan*, *didorong*, *seiring*, *hingga* and similar words are causal vs non-causal in Indonesian news; this needs an in-house annotated sample.
